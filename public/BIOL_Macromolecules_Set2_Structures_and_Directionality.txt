What three components make a nucleotide?	A phosphate group, a pentose sugar, and a nitrogenous base.
What is the name of a nucleic-acid building block containing phosphate, pentose, and base?	Nucleotide.
Which pentose sugar occurs in DNA?	Deoxyribose.
Which nucleic acid contains deoxyribose?	DNA.
Which pentose sugar occurs in RNA?	Ribose.
Which nucleic acid contains ribose?	RNA.
Which bases occur in DNA?	A, T, G, and C.
Which bases occur in RNA?	A, U, G, and C.
Which base in RNA takes the place of thymine in the usual base list?	Uracil (U).
Which sugar position distinguishes ribose from deoxyribose?	The 2′ carbon.
What is attached to the 2′ carbon in ribose that is absent at that position in deoxyribose?	An –OH group; deoxyribose has H instead.
A nucleotide's sugar has H rather than OH at 2′. Which course nucleic acid does it match?	DNA.
A nucleotide's sugar has OH at 2′. Which course nucleic acid does it match?	RNA.
Which pentose carbon is attached to the nitrogenous base?	1′.
What component attaches to the sugar at 1′?	The nitrogenous base.
Which pentose carbon bears the OH needed at the growing end of a nucleic-acid strand?	3′.
Why is the terminal 3′-OH important?	It is needed to form the next backbone linkage during chain extension.
Which numbered sugar carbon is a ring carbon connected to the outside-ring 5′ carbon?	4′.
Which numbered pentose carbon lies outside the ring and is commonly attached to phosphate?	5′.
A sugar diagram shows CH₂ outside the ring connected to phosphate. Which carbon is this?	5′.
What does the prime mark in 3′ indicate in this context?	Sugar-carbon numbering, distinguished from numbering in the base.
What defines the 5′ end of a nucleic-acid strand?	The end corresponding to the terminal sugar's 5′ position, often bearing phosphate.
What defines the 3′ end of a nucleic-acid strand?	The end corresponding to the terminal sugar's 3′ position, normally bearing a free 3′-OH.
To which end is a nucleotide added during normal nucleic-acid synthesis?	The 3′ end.
In which direction is a nucleic-acid strand synthesized?	5′→3′.
Why is extension at the 3′ end described as 5′→3′ synthesis?	The chain grows from its existing 5′ end toward its extending 3′ end.
If the growing terminal nucleotide lacks a usable 3′-OH, what happens to normal extension?	It stops because the next backbone linkage cannot form there.
A strand reads 5′–A–G–C–3′. At which displayed end is the next nucleotide added?	The right-hand end, at the terminal 3′-OH.
A strand is drawn 3′ on the left and 5′ on the right. Which end can extend?	The left-hand 3′ end.
Does rotating a strand diagram change its chemical 5′→3′ direction?	No; direction is determined by the end chemistry, not page orientation.
What does antiparallel mean for DNA's two strands?	They run in opposite 5′→3′ directions.
One DNA strand runs 5′→3′ from left to right. How does the other run from left to right?	3′→5′.
What does polymer polarity mean when discussing 5′ and 3′ ends?	The polymer has chemically different ends and therefore a direction.
Does polymer polarity mean that one DNA end is positive and the other is negative?	No; it refers here to chemically different ends.
Which linkage holds successive nucleotides together within one strand?	Phosphodiester linkage.
Which components alternate along a nucleic-acid backbone?	Sugars and phosphates.
Are nitrogenous bases the repeating links of the sugar–phosphate backbone?	No; they attach to the sugars and project from the backbone.
What four groups attach to an amino acid's α-carbon in the general course structure?	An amino group, a carboxyl group, H, and an R group.
Which part of an amino acid varies and determines many of its properties?	The R group, or side chain.
What is the α-carbon in the general amino-acid structure?	The central carbon bonded to the amino group, carboxyl group, H, and R group.
Identify the building block H₂N–CH(R)–COOH.	An amino acid in its neutral schematic form.
Draw the general neutral schematic structure of an amino acid.	H₂N–CH(R)–COOH.
How can the amino and carboxyl groups appear in an ionized amino-acid drawing?	As –NH₃⁺ and –COO⁻.
If two amino acids have different R groups, must their general backbone groups differ?	No; the R groups vary while the general backbone arrangement is shared.
Which end of a polypeptide has the free backbone amino group?	The N-terminus.
Which end of a polypeptide has the free backbone carboxyl group?	The C-terminus.
In which direction are protein sequences conventionally written?	N-terminus→C-terminus.
To which end are new amino acids added during normal protein synthesis?	The C-terminal end.
A peptide is drawn with its N-terminus on the right. Which end normally grows?	The left-hand C-terminal end.
Which groups connect when two amino acids form a peptide linkage in the course model?	The carboxyl group of one and amino group of the other.
What reaction type breaks a peptide chain into smaller pieces using water?	Hydrolysis.
What reaction type describes joining free amino acids with net loss of water?	Condensation/dehydration in the simplified course model.
How many peptide bonds join three amino-acid residues in one unbranched chain?	Two.
How many peptide bonds join n amino-acid residues in one unbranched chain?	n − 1.
Does an amino group on a side chain define the N-terminus?	No; the N-terminus is the free amino end of the backbone.
Compare the extending ends of nucleic acids and proteins.	Nucleic acids extend at the 3′ end; proteins extend at the C-terminal end.
What simple sugar building blocks form disaccharides and polysaccharides?	Monosaccharides.
To which broad biomolecule class does a monosaccharide belong?	Carbohydrates.
Which linkage connects the sugar units of a disaccharide?	Glycosidic linkage.
Which disaccharide is made from two glucose units?	Maltose.
Which monosaccharides make maltose?	Glucose + glucose.
Which disaccharide contains glucose and galactose?	Lactose.
Which monosaccharides make lactose?	Glucose + galactose.
Which disaccharide contains glucose and fructose?	Sucrose.
Which monosaccharides make sucrose?	Glucose + fructose.
Complete hydrolysis produces glucose and galactose from one course disaccharide. Which one?	Lactose.
Complete hydrolysis produces glucose and fructose from one course disaccharide. Which one?	Sucrose.
Which polysaccharide is used for glucose storage in plants?	Starch.
Which main-chain linkage is characteristic of starch?	α(1→4).
Which branched component of starch contains α(1→6) branch points?	Amylopectin.
Which linkage forms the main chains of amylopectin?	α(1→4).
Which linkage marks the branch points of amylopectin?	α(1→6).
Are starch and amylopectin entirely separate categories?	No; amylopectin is a branched component of starch.
Which glucose polymer contains β(1→4) linkages?	Cellulose.
What is cellulose's main course-level biological role?	Structural support in plant cell walls.
Which plant polysaccharide forms long structural fibres?	Cellulose.
What linkage difference distinguishes starch main chains from cellulose?	Starch has α(1→4); cellulose has β(1→4).
Do starch and cellulose differ because one uses glucose and the other does not?	No; both use glucose, but their linkage configurations differ.
A plant glucose polymer has α(1→4) chains and α(1→6) branches. Identify it.	Amylopectin.
A plant glucose polymer has β(1→4) linkages and forms fibres. Identify it.	Cellulose.
Why can two polymers made from glucose have different biological roles?	Different linkage geometries produce different structures and enzyme recognition.
An enzyme recognizes α(1→4) linkages. Does that guarantee it can cleave β(1→4)?	No; the different geometry may prevent recognition.
An enzyme cleaves only α(1→4) bonds in amylopectin. Which branch-point linkage remains uncleaved by it?	α(1→6).
After complete hydrolysis, both starch and cellulose yield glucose. Does that establish that their linkages were identical?	No; monomer identity does not specify linkage geometry.
What distinguishes a branch connection in amylopectin from its main-chain connection?	The second glucose contributes C6 at a branch instead of C4.

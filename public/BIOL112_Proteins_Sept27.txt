What geometry surrounds the α-carbon of a standard amino acid?	Tetrahedral: its four single bonds point in three-dimensional directions.
A free amino acid has NH₃⁺ and COO⁻ groups. Can those two groups alone tell you whether its R group is nonpolar?	No. They belong to the common backbone; inspect the side chain separately.
In a water-soluble globular protein, where would you usually expect nonpolar R groups?	Buried in the interior, reducing contact between nonpolar surfaces and water.
Would a nonpolar R group facing lipid tails in a membrane protein necessarily be misplaced?	No. A lipid-facing nonpolar surface is favorable; the “buried core” rule assumes an aqueous environment.
What weak attractions help stabilize closely packed nonpolar side chains after they come together?	Van der Waals attractions between nearby atoms.
A negatively charged R group lies near a positively charged R group. What interaction can stabilize their association?	An ionic interaction (salt bridge).
How can changing pH disrupt an ionic interaction between two R groups?	Changing protonation can change the charges needed for attraction.
Which amino acid has H as its R group?	Glycine; its α-carbon is attached to two hydrogens.
An amino-acid side chain closes a ring by bonding back to the backbone nitrogen. Which amino acid is it?	Proline.
Why does glycine allow more backbone flexibility than proline?	Glycine has a tiny H side chain; proline’s ring constrains backbone rotation.
Which special amino acid in this reading has a –CH₂–SH side chain?	Cysteine.
How can two cysteine side chains covalently crosslink parts of a protein?	Their thiol groups can be oxidized to form an S–S disulfide bond, not S=S.
Why is rotation around the peptide C–N bond restricted?	Resonance gives it partial double-bond character, making it shorter and more rigid than an ordinary C–N single bond.
If the peptide C–N bond is rigid, which backbone bonds still allow a polypeptide to change conformation?	The N–Cα and Cα–carbonyl-C bonds can rotate, within steric constraints.
Do N–Gly–Cys–Pro–C and N–Pro–Cys–Gly–C have the same primary structure?	No. They have the same composition but different amino-acid order from N to C.
Why can replacing one amino acid change a protein’s function?	The new R group can alter interactions and folding, which may change the functional site.
Which backbone groups form the hydrogen bonds that stabilize α-helices and β-sheets?	A carbonyl oxygen accepts a hydrogen bond from a backbone N–H group.
A polypeptide segment forms a regular coil stabilized by backbone hydrogen bonds. Which secondary structure is it?	An α-helix.
In a typical α-helix, where do the R groups project?	Outward from the helix axis, rather than into its backbone hydrogen-bonded core.
Extended polypeptide strands align side by side and hydrogen-bond between their backbones. Which secondary structure results?	A β-sheet; the arrangement is pleated rather than helical.
What is the key interaction distinction between secondary and tertiary protein structure?	Secondary structure uses local backbone hydrogen bonding; tertiary folding involves many interactions, especially among R groups.
One continuous polypeptide folds into several helices and sheets. What level describes its overall 3D shape?	Tertiary structure; several folded regions do not by themselves mean several subunits.
Two identical polypeptide chains associate into a functional protein. Does it have quaternary structure?	Yes. Quaternary structure requires multiple polypeptides, whether identical or different.
A two-subunit protein separates into two still-folded chains. Which structural level has been lost?	Quaternary structure; each chain can retain its tertiary structure.
How does a disulfide within one chain differ structurally from one joining two subunits?	The first stabilizes tertiary folding; the second can stabilize quaternary association.
A protein unfolds on heating without breaking peptide bonds. Which structural level is retained?	Primary structure: the amino-acid sequence remains intact.
Why can denaturation abolish binding even when the amino-acid sequence is unchanged?	The binding site may lose the 3D shape and chemical arrangement needed to fit its partner.
Why are unfolded proteins often more prone to aggregation?	Normally buried hydrophobic regions become exposed and associate with those of other proteins.
How can a chaperone reduce inappropriate aggregation during folding?	It temporarily shields exposed hydrophobic regions, allowing the polypeptide an opportunity to fold correctly.
Can a folding chaperone repair a protein’s incorrect amino-acid sequence by changing a residue?	No. Chaperones assist folding; they do not rewrite the primary sequence.
At approximately pH 7, rank the side chains of aspartate, threonine, and phenylalanine from most to least hydrophilic.	Aspartate (charged) > threonine (polar, uncharged) > phenylalanine (nonpolar).
A threonine side chain has an –OH group but no formal charge. Which interaction with water does that group enable?	Hydrogen bonding through its polar hydroxyl group.
Why should peptide-bond synthesis not be treated like spontaneous phospholipid-bilayer assembly?	Peptide synthesis requires energy input and ribosomal catalysis; bilayer assembly organizes existing lipids.
Which atoms form the repeating protein backbone, excluding the R groups?	The repeating N–Cα–carbonyl-C chain.
Why is glycine the only non-chiral common amino acid in the lecture?	Its α-carbon has two identical H substituents rather than four different groups.
What does an amino-acid residue mean in a polypeptide?	The amino-acid-derived unit remaining after incorporation into the peptide chain.
A protein speeds up a cellular chemical reaction. Which listed protein role is it performing?	Catalysis: it is acting as an enzyme.
In Lecture 10’s introductory terminology, what distinguishes a protein from just a long polypeptide chain?	The protein has folded into a functional structure; chain length alone does not establish function.

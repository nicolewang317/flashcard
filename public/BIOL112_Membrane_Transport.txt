What membrane property helps maintain cellular homeostasis?	Selective permeability.
Which passes through a lipid bilayer more easily: O₂ or Na⁺?	O₂; it is small and nonpolar.
Why does adding aquaporins increase water transport across a membrane?	Water can cross the lipid bilayer slowly; aquaporin channels provide a much faster pathway.
Why do ions usually need transport proteins to cross a lipid bilayer?	The hydrophobic interior is energetically unfavorable for charged, hydrated ions.
What two membrane components together determine selective permeability?	The lipid bilayer and membrane proteins.
How does a carrier move a solute differently from a channel?	A carrier binds solute and alternates access by changing conformation; a channel provides a selective pore.
Does movement through a membrane protein automatically mean active transport?	No; facilitated diffusion also uses proteins.
What distinguishes simple from facilitated diffusion?	Simple diffusion crosses the lipid bilayer; facilitated diffusion uses a membrane protein.
At diffusion equilibrium, do molecules stop moving?	No; movement continues in both directions with no net movement.
What distinguishes active transport from passive transport?	Active transport couples an energy source to uphill solute movement; passive transport moves downhill. For ions, use the electrochemical gradient.
What directly powers the Na⁺/K⁺ pump in a neuron or other animal cell?	ATP hydrolysis powers its cycle of conformational changes: primary active transport.
Why can secondary active transport be active even without direct ATP use?	Downhill ion movement along its electrochemical gradient is coupled to uphill transport of another solute.
What two differences make up an electrochemical gradient?	Ion concentration difference and electrical potential difference.
A membrane passes water but not solute. Side A has more solute than B, with equal pressure. Which way is net water flow?	From B to A, toward the higher concentration of nonpenetrating solute.
An animal cell enters a hypotonic solution. What happens to its volume?	Water enters by osmosis; the cell swells and may lyse.
Why do ion pumps help cells regulate their volume?	Ion concentrations affect osmotic water movement.
What does a contractile vacuole do in a freshwater protist?	Collects and expels excess water.
Why does a plant cell wall limit osmotic swelling?	As water enters, the wall resists expansion; rising turgor pressure opposes further net water entry.
Why does glucose cross a protein-free lipid bilayer much less readily than O₂?	Glucose is larger and has many polar hydroxyl groups; entering the hydrophobic core is unfavorable.
Two fixed 1-L compartments contain 8 mM and 2 mM of a freely permeating neutral solute. With no reactions, what concentration is reached on each side?	5 mM on each side: total solute is conserved and concentrations become equal.
For neutral glucose transported by GLUT, what happens to net flux if the glucose concentration gradient reverses?	Net glucose flux reverses; GLUT mediates facilitated diffusion down the gradient.
Opening a gated ion channel allows flux. What determines its net direction?	The ion’s electrochemical gradient; opening the gate does not provide energy for uphill pumping.
In one normal Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase cycle, what crosses the membrane per ATP hydrolyzed?	3 Na⁺ move out and 2 K⁺ move in.
A pump moves 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in per cycle. What is its net charge transfer?	One positive charge moves outward per cycle.
Na⁺ and glucose enter an intestinal epithelial cell together. Identify the cotransport pattern.	Symport: the two substances cross in the same direction; antiport means opposite directions.
A positive ion is more concentrated outside, but the inside is strongly positive. Can concentration alone predict net channel flux?	No. The inward chemical drive competes with the outward electrical drive; their combined effect determines net flux.
After Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase inhibition, the inward Na⁺ driving force gradually collapses. Why does uphill glucose uptake by Na⁺–glucose symport weaken?	The cotransporter loses the downhill Na⁺ energy that powers uphill glucose transport.
Which can maintain uphill glucose transport: GLUT or a Na⁺–glucose cotransporter supplied with a sufficient inward Na⁺ gradient?	The Na⁺–glucose cotransporter uses ion-gradient energy; GLUT alone permits only downhill glucose flux.
In one second, 100 molecules cross A → B and 60 cross B → A. What is the net movement?	40 molecules A → B; individual molecules still cross both ways.
GLUT transports glucose without obligatorily coupling it to a second solute. Is it a uniporter, symporter, or antiporter?	A uniporter: one solute species is transported without obligatory cotransport of another.
Does downhill solute movement automatically produce ATP or drive another solute uphill?	No. Downhill movement releases free energy; a coupling mechanism is needed to capture it for work, such as uphill cotransport.
Why should “electrochemical gradient” not be replaced by “action potential” in a transport explanation?	An electrochemical gradient is a driving force from concentration and voltage differences; an action potential is a transient change in membrane voltage.
Sketch a symporter and an antiporter across a membrane, using arrows for solutes A and B.	Symporter: A and B cross in the same direction. Antiporter: they cross in opposite directions. Compare your arrows with the diagrams.
Na⁺ enters a heart muscle cell as Ca²⁺ leaves via one exchanger. Identify the coupled transport pattern.	Antiport: Na⁺ enters while Ca²⁺ leaves through the Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger.
O₂ enters a red blood cell directly through its lipid bilayer. Which transport mechanism is this?	Simple diffusion: a small nonpolar molecule crosses the lipid bilayer down its gradient.
A human red blood cell takes up glucose down its gradient through GLUT1. Which transport mechanism is this?	Carrier-mediated facilitated diffusion; GLUT1 provides the route without pumping glucose uphill.
Why are polar or charged R groups expected along a water-filled ion-channel passage?	They interact favorably with water and transported ions, unlike the surrounding lipid-facing surface.
How does a leak channel differ from a gated channel in the lecture model?	A leak channel is open; a gated channel opens or closes in response to a signal.
Why can channel transport usually be faster than carrier transport?	An open pore lets solutes pass without a separate alternating-access cycle for each solute.
For neutral glucose, [inside] = 1 mM and [outside] = 10 mM. What is the inside/outside concentration ratio?	0.1; the initial concentration gradient favors inward movement.
Sketch the inside/outside glucose ratio over time for passive uptake starting below 1, with no glucose metabolism or other transport.	The ratio rises toward 1 and levels off there; passive uptake alone does not maintain a ratio above 1.
These neutral-glucose curves start below 1. With no metabolism or binding, which curve requires energy-coupled accumulation?	A: its inside/outside concentration ratio rises above 1, accumulating glucose against its gradient.
Can a neutral-solute concentration-ratio curve approaching 1, by itself, distinguish simple from facilitated diffusion?	No. Both can equilibrate concentrations; identify the molecule and whether a membrane protein provides the route.
Draw neutral-solute particles in equal-volume compartments when [inside]/[outside] = 3.	Show three times as many particles inside as outside, for example 9 inside and 3 outside.
Which amino-acid side chain is transiently phosphorylated in the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase example?	An aspartate side chain.
Why is energy-coupled glucose uptake useful in intestinal cells when luminal glucose becomes scarce?	It can continue uptake against the glucose concentration gradient, reducing loss of available glucose.

## 1. Amino-acid geometry

**Front:** What geometry surrounds the α-carbon of a standard amino acid?

**Back:** Tetrahedral: its four single bonds point in three-dimensional directions.

## 2. Backbone versus side chain

**Front:** A free amino acid has NH₃⁺ and COO⁻ groups. Can those two groups alone tell you whether its R group is nonpolar?

**Back:** No. They belong to the common backbone; inspect the side chain separately.

## 3. Hydrophobic core

**Front:** In a water-soluble globular protein, where would you usually expect nonpolar R groups?

**Back:** Buried in the interior, reducing contact between nonpolar surfaces and water.

## 4. Environment and folding

**Front:** Would a nonpolar R group facing lipid tails in a membrane protein necessarily be misplaced?

**Back:** No. A lipid-facing nonpolar surface is favorable; the “buried core” rule assumes an aqueous environment.

## 5. Close packing

**Front:** What weak attractions help stabilize closely packed nonpolar side chains after they come together?

**Back:** Van der Waals attractions between nearby atoms.

## 6. Ionic side-chain interactions

**Front:** A negatively charged R group lies near a positively charged R group. What interaction can stabilize their association?

**Back:** An ionic interaction (salt bridge).

## 7. pH and folding

**Front:** How can changing pH disrupt an ionic interaction between two R groups?

**Back:** Changing protonation can change the charges needed for attraction.

## 8. Glycine structure

**Front:** Which amino acid has H as its R group?

**Back:** Glycine; its α-carbon is attached to two hydrogens.

## 9. Proline structure

**Front:** An amino-acid side chain closes a ring by bonding back to the backbone nitrogen. Which amino acid is it?

**Back:** Proline.

## 10. Side-chain geometry and flexibility

**Front:** Why does glycine allow more backbone flexibility than proline?

**Back:** Glycine has a tiny H side chain; proline’s ring constrains backbone rotation.

## 11. Cysteine structure

**Front:** Which special amino acid in this reading has a –CH₂–SH side chain?

**Back:** Cysteine.

## 12. Disulfide formation

**Front:** How can two cysteine side chains covalently crosslink parts of a protein?

**Back:** Their thiol groups can be oxidized to form an S–S disulfide bond, not S=S.

## 13. Peptide-bond resonance

**Front:** Why is rotation around the peptide C–N bond restricted?

**Back:** Resonance gives it partial double-bond character, making it shorter and more rigid than an ordinary C–N single bond.

## 14. Backbone flexibility

**Front:** If the peptide C–N bond is rigid, which backbone bonds still allow a polypeptide to change conformation?

**Back:** The N–Cα and Cα–carbonyl-C bonds can rotate, within steric constraints.

## 15. Primary structure

**Front:** Do N–Gly–Cys–Pro–C and N–Pro–Cys–Gly–C have the same primary structure?

**Back:** No. They have the same composition but different amino-acid order from N to C.

## 16. Sequence → folding → function

**Front:** Why can replacing one amino acid change a protein’s function?

**Back:** The new R group can alter interactions and folding, which may change the functional site.

## 17. Secondary-structure hydrogen bonds

**Front:** Which backbone groups form the hydrogen bonds that stabilize α-helices and β-sheets?

**Back:** A carbonyl oxygen accepts a hydrogen bond from a backbone N–H group.

## 18. α-Helix recognition

**Front:** A polypeptide segment forms a regular coil stabilized by backbone hydrogen bonds. Which secondary structure is it?

**Back:** An α-helix.

## 19. α-Helix side-chain placement

**Front:** In a typical α-helix, where do the R groups project?

**Back:** Outward from the helix axis, rather than into its backbone hydrogen-bonded core.

## 20. β-Sheet recognition

**Front:** Extended polypeptide strands align side by side and hydrogen-bond between their backbones. Which secondary structure results?

**Back:** A β-sheet; the arrangement is pleated rather than helical.

## 21. Secondary versus tertiary

**Front:** What is the key interaction distinction between secondary and tertiary protein structure?

**Back:** Secondary structure uses local backbone hydrogen bonding; tertiary folding involves many interactions, especially among R groups.

## 22. One-chain tertiary structure

**Front:** One continuous polypeptide folds into several helices and sheets. What level describes its overall 3D shape?

**Back:** Tertiary structure; several folded regions do not by themselves mean several subunits.

## 23. Quaternary assembly

**Front:** Two identical polypeptide chains associate into a functional protein. Does it have quaternary structure?

**Back:** Yes. Quaternary structure requires multiple polypeptides, whether identical or different.

## 24. Disassembly of subunits

**Front:** A two-subunit protein separates into two still-folded chains. Which structural level has been lost?

**Back:** Quaternary structure; each chain can retain its tertiary structure.

## 25. Disulfides across structural levels

**Front:** How does a disulfide within one chain differ structurally from one joining two subunits?

**Back:** The first stabilizes tertiary folding; the second can stabilize quaternary association.

## 26. Denaturation versus backbone cleavage

**Front:** A protein unfolds on heating without breaking peptide bonds. Which structural level is retained?

**Back:** Primary structure: the amino-acid sequence remains intact.

## 27. Shape-dependent function

**Front:** Why can denaturation abolish binding even when the amino-acid sequence is unchanged?

**Back:** The binding site may lose the 3D shape and chemical arrangement needed to fit its partner.

## 28. Unfolding and aggregation

**Front:** Why are unfolded proteins often more prone to aggregation?

**Back:** Normally buried hydrophobic regions become exposed and associate with those of other proteins.

## 29. Chaperone mechanism

**Front:** How can a chaperone reduce inappropriate aggregation during folding?

**Back:** It temporarily shields exposed hydrophobic regions, allowing the polypeptide an opportunity to fold correctly.

## 30. Limits of folding assistance

**Front:** Can a folding chaperone repair a protein’s incorrect amino-acid sequence by changing a residue?

**Back:** No. Chaperones assist folding; they do not rewrite the primary sequence.
